Interest Only Mortgage Calculator UK

Understand your monthly mortgage interest payments, outstanding capital and repayment options. Explore residential interest-only mortgages, part-and-part mortgages and buy-to-let interest-only mortgages with this comprehensive UK mortgage guide.

By Fintrava UK Mortgage Education Updated: November 2026

An interest-only mortgage can offer lower monthly repayments compared with a traditional capital repayment mortgage. However, there is an important difference: your monthly payments cover the interest charged on the loan, while the original amount borrowed remains outstanding.

Whether you are buying your own home, considering a part-and-part mortgage or financing a rental property, understanding your mortgage structure is essential.

The Interest Only Mortgage Calculator UK helps you understand the relationship between your mortgage balance, interest rate, monthly interest payments and outstanding capital.

Important principle: Paying only the interest does not automatically reduce your mortgage debt. You still need a credible strategy to repay the outstanding capital by the end of the mortgage term.

2. What Is an Interest-Only Mortgage?

An interest-only mortgage is a type of home loan where your regular monthly mortgage payment covers the interest charged by the lender rather than repaying the borrowed capital.

For example, if you borrow £200,000 at an annual interest rate of 5%, a simplified interest-only calculation gives a monthly interest payment of approximately £833.33.

However, the £200,000 capital remains outstanding. At the end of the mortgage term, you must repay that amount through an appropriate repayment strategy or another agreed arrangement.

Interest-only vs repayment mortgage

Feature Interest-Only Repayment
Monthly payment Interest only Capital and interest
Capital reduction Not through normal interest payments Yes
Outstanding balance Generally remains unchanged Gradually decreases
End of mortgage term Capital still needs repayment Normally fully repaid
Repayment strategy Essential Built into monthly payments

4. Interest-Only Mortgage Calculation Formula

The basic interest-only mortgage formula is relatively simple. It estimates the monthly interest based on the outstanding capital and annual interest rate.

Monthly Interest Payment

Monthly Interest = (Mortgage Balance × Annual Interest Rate) ÷ 12

Example

Mortgage Balance = £200,000
Annual Interest Rate = 5%
Monthly Interest = (£200,000 × 0.05) ÷ 12
Monthly Interest = £833.33

This is a simplified estimate assuming a constant balance and annual rate with monthly interest calculation. Actual lender payments may differ because of daily interest, payment dates, fees and product-specific terms.

5. Residential Interest-Only Mortgage Calculator

A residential interest-only mortgage is a mortgage secured against a property that you intend to use as your main home or residential property.

Instead of repaying the borrowed capital through every monthly payment, you pay the interest due. The outstanding capital must be repaid by the end of the agreed mortgage term.

Example: Residential interest-only mortgage

Mortgage detail Illustrative value
Mortgage amount£250,000
Interest rate5%
Mortgage term25 years
Monthly interest£1,041.67
Capital repaid through interest payments£0
Capital remaining at term end£250,000

This illustration assumes the interest rate and outstanding balance remain unchanged for the entire term. It excludes fees and any separate capital repayments.

Who might consider residential interest-only?

Some borrowers may consider interest-only arrangements where their financial circumstances and repayment strategy meet the lender's requirements.

Lenders assess affordability and the proposed repayment strategy. A borrower cannot assume that an interest-only residential mortgage will be available simply because the monthly interest payment is affordable.

Capital repayment warning: A residential interest-only mortgage does not automatically pay off your home. You need a credible plan to repay the full outstanding capital by the end of the mortgage term.

6. Part-and-Part Mortgage Calculator

A part-and-part mortgage combines two repayment structures within one mortgage:

  • Capital repayment portion: You repay part of the borrowed capital and interest through regular monthly payments.
  • Interest-only portion: You pay interest on the remaining portion while the capital remains outstanding.

This structure allows borrowers to divide the mortgage between a repayment component and an interest-only component.

How does a part-and-part mortgage work?

Imagine you borrow £200,000 and split the mortgage into two equal portions.

Mortgage portion Amount
Capital repayment portion£100,000
Interest-only portion£100,000
Total mortgage£200,000

The first £100,000 is repaid gradually through capital and interest payments. The other £100,000 continues to incur interest without being repaid through its normal interest-only payment.

Part-and-part calculation example

Assume a £200,000 mortgage at 5% annual interest over 25 years, split equally between repayment and interest-only.

Interest-only portion

£100,000 × 5% ÷ 12 = £416.67 per month

Repayment portion

Monthly repayment on £100,000 over 25 years at 5% ≈ £584.59

Combined estimated payment

£416.67 + £584.59 = £1,001.26 per month

At the end of the 25-year term, assuming all scheduled repayment payments have been made, the repayment portion should be cleared, but the £100,000 interest-only capital will still need to be repaid.

This is an illustrative calculation using a constant monthly interest-rate model. It excludes fees and lender-specific calculation differences.

Advantages and considerations

  • Combines capital reduction with lower payments than a fully repayment-based mortgage may require.
  • Can provide flexibility in structuring the borrowed capital.
  • Leaves an outstanding interest-only balance that still needs a repayment strategy.
  • May require lender approval and affordability assessment when changing an existing mortgage.

7. Buy-to-Let Interest-Only Mortgage Calculator

A buy-to-let mortgage is designed for property purchased primarily to rent to tenants rather than to occupy as your own home.

Some landlords use interest-only mortgages because the monthly payments can be lower than those on an equivalent capital repayment mortgage.

However, lower monthly payments do not remove the obligation to repay the original capital. The outstanding mortgage balance remains payable at the end of the agreed term.

Buy-to-let interest calculation example

Mortgage detail Illustrative value
Property mortgage£180,000
Annual interest rate5.5%
Mortgage term20 years
Monthly interest£825
Annual interest£9,900
Outstanding capital at term end£180,000

The example assumes an unchanged interest rate and mortgage balance throughout the term. It does not include property maintenance, tax, insurance, letting-agent costs or other expenses.

What should landlords consider?

  • Expected rental income.
  • Mortgage interest costs.
  • Property maintenance and repairs.
  • Insurance and letting-agent fees.
  • Periods when the property may be vacant.
  • Interest rate changes.
  • Rental income tax treatment.
  • How the mortgage capital will be repaid.

Rental income is not the same as profit

A property generating £1,500 in monthly rent does not necessarily produce £1,500 in profit. Mortgage payments, repairs, insurance, tax, management expenses and empty periods can all affect the final result.

Landlords should also check lender-specific rental income assessment and interest coverage requirements. These can differ by lender and mortgage product.

Important: Do not judge a buy-to-let investment only by comparing rent with the monthly interest payment. Assess the full property cash flow, tax position, financing conditions and capital repayment strategy.

9. How Do You Repay an Interest-Only Mortgage?

The most important part of an interest-only mortgage is having a credible repayment strategy. You need to arrange how the outstanding capital will be repaid before the mortgage term ends.

Depending on the mortgage product and lender, possible repayment resources may include:

  • Cash savings.
  • Stocks and shares ISAs.
  • Other investments.
  • Pension-related funds where permitted and suitable.
  • Sale of another property or asset.
  • Other acceptable repayment resources.

Lenders decide which repayment strategies they will accept. A projected inheritance or a speculative increase in property value should not be treated as a guaranteed repayment plan.

How much might you need to save?

If you need to accumulate a target amount by the end of your mortgage, you can estimate the monthly savings required.

For example, if you have a £100,000 interest-only balance and 20 years remaining, you need to consider how to build sufficient funds to meet that obligation.

The amount you need to save depends on the starting savings balance, expected returns, fees, tax treatment and investment risk.

Investment returns are not guaranteed. The value of investments can fall as well as rise. Review your repayment strategy regularly rather than assuming it will automatically cover the mortgage.

10. Risks of Interest-Only Mortgages

Interest-only mortgages can offer lower monthly payments, but they also create an important long-term repayment responsibility.

Outstanding capital risk

The original capital does not disappear. If you borrow £250,000 and only make interest payments, you may still owe £250,000 at the end of the mortgage term.

Investment performance risk

If your repayment strategy relies on investments, the final value may be lower than expected. You could face a shortfall when the mortgage becomes due.

Remortgage risk

You should not assume that you will automatically qualify for a new mortgage at the end of your existing deal. Future lending criteria, income and property values may affect your options.

Property value risk

If property values fall while your mortgage capital remains unchanged, your equity may decrease. This can make refinancing or selling the property more difficult.

11. Can You Switch from Interest-Only to Repayment?

Some borrowers with an existing interest-only mortgage may want to switch to a repayment mortgage or a part-and-part arrangement.

You should contact your mortgage provider to discuss the options available under your current agreement.

Your lender may assess:

  • Your current income and affordability.
  • Your outstanding mortgage balance.
  • Your remaining mortgage term.
  • Your age and expected retirement plans.
  • Your proposed monthly repayment amount.
  • Your mortgage product and applicable fees.

An increase in monthly payments may require an affordability assessment. Approval is not guaranteed.

13. Interest-Only vs Full Repayment: Which Is Better?

Neither structure is automatically better for every borrower. The appropriate mortgage depends on your financial circumstances, property objectives and repayment strategy.

Consideration Interest-Only Full Repayment
Initial monthly payment Generally lower Generally higher
Capital reduction Separate strategy needed Built into payments
End-of-term balance Capital remains due Normally zero
Long-term planning Requires a separate plan Scheduled capital repayment
Investment risk May apply to repayment strategy Depends on separate investments

The lower monthly cost of an interest-only mortgage should not be confused with a lower total cost of borrowing. You need to consider all interest payments, fees and the final capital repayment.

15. Frequently Asked Questions

What is an interest-only mortgage?

An interest-only mortgage is a loan where your regular monthly payments cover the interest charged on the outstanding capital. You must repay the capital separately by the end of the mortgage term.

How do I calculate monthly interest-only mortgage payments?

Multiply the outstanding mortgage balance by the annual interest rate and divide by 12 for a simplified estimate. Actual lender calculations may differ.

What is a residential interest-only mortgage?

It is an interest-only mortgage secured against a residential property that the borrower intends to occupy as a home. The outstanding capital still needs to be repaid at the end of the term.

What is a part-and-part mortgage?

A part-and-part mortgage combines a capital repayment portion and an interest-only portion within the same mortgage. The interest-only portion remains outstanding until it is repaid.

What is a buy-to-let interest-only mortgage?

It is a mortgage used to finance a rental property where the monthly mortgage payment generally covers interest, rather than repaying the full capital. The borrowed capital remains due.

Is an interest-only mortgage cheaper?

Its monthly payment is generally lower than an equivalent repayment mortgage because the monthly payment does not include scheduled capital repayment. However, the capital remains outstanding and the total borrowing cost can be higher.

Do I need a repayment strategy?

Yes. A credible repayment strategy is important because the outstanding capital must be repaid by the end of the mortgage term. Lenders assess repayment strategies under applicable lending rules.

Can I change from interest-only to part-and-part?

You can ask your lender whether it permits a change to a part-and-part structure. Affordability checks, product conditions and other requirements may apply.

Can I make overpayments on an interest-only mortgage?

Some lenders permit capital overpayments on interest-only mortgages. Check your mortgage conditions, annual allowances and any applicable early repayment charges.

What happens when an interest-only mortgage ends?

The outstanding capital becomes due. You need to repay it using your agreed repayment resources or another arrangement accepted by the lender.

Does buy-to-let rental income automatically cover the mortgage?

No. Rental income can be affected by vacancies, repairs, insurance, taxes, management expenses and interest rate changes. Assess the full property cash flow.

Are Fintrava calculator results guaranteed?

No. The calculations are estimates based on the figures and assumptions entered. Your mortgage provider's actual figures may differ.
